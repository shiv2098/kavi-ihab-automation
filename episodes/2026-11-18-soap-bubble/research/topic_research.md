# Research and fact-check — 18 November 2026

## Core mechanism
A soap bubble is a thin liquid film with air on both sides. Incoming white light is partly reflected at the outer air-to-film boundary; some enters the film, reflects at the inner film-to-air boundary and exits. The two reflected rays overlap. Their relative phase depends on optical path length, wavelength, film refractive index, film thickness and viewing angle. Some wavelengths are reinforced (constructive interference); others are reduced or cancelled (destructive interference).

For a soap film in air, reflection at the first air-to-higher-index boundary receives a half-wavelength (180°) phase reversal. Reflection at the lower film-to-air boundary does not. An extremely thin film can therefore look dark because the reflected rays are out of phase and cancel across visible wavelengths.

Film thickness is not uniform. Liquid drains under gravity and the film evolves over time; viewing angle also changes the optical path difference. Different wavelengths are therefore reinforced at different positions and angles. The light itself does not change wavelength; the film changes which wavelengths are preferentially reflected.

## Fact-check notes
- The dominant mechanism is thin-film interference, not a miniature rainbow caused by refraction through droplets.
- The bubble contains no coloured pigment.
- Do not claim every bubble displays a fixed colour order. The pattern depends on local thickness, refractive index, illumination and viewing angle.
- A dark patch is due to strong cancellation of reflected visible wavelengths, not black pigment.
- Gravity-driven drainage is a simplification; surface flows and evaporation can also change film thickness.
