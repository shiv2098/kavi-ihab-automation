Why does a soap bubble wear a rainbow—and why does it keep changing? Its skin is a thin film of soapy water. When white light hits it, some reflects from the outer surface. The rest enters the film, bounces off the inner surface, and emerges beside the first reflection. One reflection flips phase by half a cycle. The two waves overlap. Depending on film thickness and viewing angle, some wavelengths reinforce each other while others cancel. That is thin-film interference. Gravity drains liquid down the bubble, changing its thickness, so the colours shift across its surface. Just before it bursts, extremely thin patches can look dark: reflected visible wavelengths cancel. The rainbow is made by light waves adding—and subtracting.
