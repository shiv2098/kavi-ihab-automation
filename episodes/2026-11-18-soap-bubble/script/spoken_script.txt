Why does a soap bubble wear a rainbow—and why does it keep changing? Its skin is a film of soapy water, only a tiny fraction of a millimetre thick. When white light hits it, some reflects from the outer surface. The rest enters the film, bounces off the inner surface, and emerges beside the first reflection. Those two light waves overlap. Depending on the film's thickness and the viewing angle, some wavelengths reinforce each other while others cancel. That is thin-film interference. Gravity slowly drains liquid down the bubble, changing the film's thickness, so the colours shift across its surface. Then comes the eerie final clue: where the film becomes extremely thin, reflected light can cancel across the visible spectrum, leaving a dark patch. The rainbow is made by light waves adding—and subtracting.
