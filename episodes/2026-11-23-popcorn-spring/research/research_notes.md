# Research notes — 23 November 2026

## Primary study
Virot E, Ponomarenko A. (2015). *Popcorn: critical temperature, jump and sound*. Journal of the Royal Society Interface 12(104):20141247. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2014.1247. Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4345489/ . PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25673298/ .

The study used high-speed video at 2,900 frames per second and synchronized audio. Almost all kernels in its temperature tests popped near a critical temperature of 180°C. After the pericarp fractures, expanding starch forms a small “leg” that contacts and compresses against the hot surface; its rebound launches the flake. The synchronized recording showed the pop sound during the fracture/vapour-release stage before the jump. The authors propose that the sound is caused by released water vapour. In the observed setup, the vapour jet did not act as a rocket; the starch leg's rebound explained the jump.

## Kernel structure
Purdue Food Science describes the pericarp as the tough outer hull and reports an optimum moisture range of about 13–14.5% of kernel weight. A stronger pericarp helps retain moisture; heating builds pressure, and gelatinizing starch rapidly expands after rupture. Cracks or weaknesses can let moisture escape and leave kernels unpopped. Source: https://ag.purdue.edu/news/2020/02/whats-under-the-shell-of-this-popular-snack.html

## Guardrails
- The 180°C value is the critical internal temperature reported under the study's conditions, not a universal pan or microwave setting.
- “Starch spring” is an analogy for the compressed starch projection, not a literal spring.
- Do not say steam thrust launches the kernel.
- The sound explanation is the authors' proposed interpretation; retain “researchers proposed/linked” wording.
- Do not generalize every variety, kernel or cooking method.
