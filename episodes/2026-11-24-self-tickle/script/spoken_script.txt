Try to tickle your own ribs. It usually feels weak. But let someone else do the same thing, and your brain reacts differently. Before your hand moves, your motor system sends a copy of the movement command to prediction circuits. The cerebellum uses that copy to estimate where and when your fingers will touch. When the expected touch arrives, activity in touch-processing areas is reduced. Researchers call this sensory attenuation. In a 2020 human brain-imaging study, stronger communication between the cerebellum and somatosensory cortex went with stronger attenuation of self-generated touch. Another person's fingers arrive without your motor prediction, so the signal is less damped. That is a major reason self-tickling feels weak: your brain has already predicted the touch before you consciously feel it.
