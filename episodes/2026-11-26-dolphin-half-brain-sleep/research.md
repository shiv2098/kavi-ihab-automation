# Research brief
Research cut-off: 26 November 2026.

Dolphins are air-breathing mammals that must surface to inhale. EEG studies show unihemispheric slow-wave sleep (USWS): one cerebral hemisphere displays slow-wave sleep while the other shows a more wake-like pattern. In studied bottlenose dolphins and belugas, the eye contralateral to the sleeping hemisphere is more often closed or partly closed; the eye contralateral to the relatively awake hemisphere is more often open or partly open. The more active side is thought to support movement, sensory monitoring and controlled surfacing.

Fact-check:
- Established: USWS is documented by EEG in cetaceans.
- Qualified: eye-state asymmetry correlates with EEG state, but is not exact. The 2004 study reported ~75% accuracy for unilateral eye closure as an indicator in its small sample and documented brief mismatches.
- Functional interpretation: the relatively active hemisphere can help maintain behaviour and surfacing; phrase as “can help” or “is thought to help”.
- Avoid saying one hemisphere is completely switched off, the eye rule is always true, or every cetacean species has identical details.
