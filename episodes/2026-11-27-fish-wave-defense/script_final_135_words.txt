Why would thousands of tiny fish make a wave when a bird attacks? In Mexico’s sulfur springs, sulfur mollies crowd near the oxygen-rich surface. When a threat arrives, fish dive and flick their tails. Neighbors copy the movement, so a ripple of dives spreads across the shoal. The fish are not travelling across the pond as one giant body—the wave is a pattern passed from fish to fish. Experiments found that these displays made birds wait about twice as long before attacking again, and reduced capture success for one predator. But the birds are adapting too. A 2026 field study found kingfishers often attacked near the shoal’s edges, where fewer waves were triggered. The fish, in turn, produced stronger waves when danger returned nearby. It is a moving arms race—built without a leader.
