# Research brief — Why sulfur mollies make waves when birds attack

**Research date:** 27 November 2026  
**Lead topic:** Collective anti-predator waves in sulfur mollies (*Poecilia sulphuraria*)

## Established evidence
1. Sulfur mollies in sulfur-rich springs in Tabasco, Mexico, form dense shoals near the water surface.
2. Under threat, fish perform repeated diving movements with tail flicks/surface splashes. Nearby fish copy the movement, creating a wave-like pattern that propagates across the shoal.
3. Doran et al. (2021), in *Current Biology*, found that experimentally induced fish waves roughly doubled the time birds waited before their next attack. Capture probability also fell with wave number for one predator.
4. The precise sensory explanation for why the waves work is not fully settled. Confusion, conspicuous signalling that the shoal has detected the predator, and changes in predator approach/attack strategy remain possible explanations.
5. Pacher et al. (2026), in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, analysed nearly 800 attacks by three bird species. Kingfishers preferentially attacked shoal edges, where collective waves were weaker; the fish also showed stronger waves when attacks recurred close together (described as collective priming). Great kiskadees used a different, faster overflight strategy.
6. A 26 November 2026 *Science* Perspective proposes that wave-like collective displays across several taxa may exploit predator visual processing. This is a broader evolutionary hypothesis and calls for further tests; it does not prove every animal wave evolved for deception.

## Fact-check notes
- Use “sulfur mollies” or “sulphur mollies”; species: *Poecilia sulphuraria*.
- “Oxygen-rich surface” is a useful habitat explanation; do not claim the fish breathe air.
- The wave is an emergent pattern created by sequential local responses. Do not imply a leader coordinates the shoal.
- “About twice as long between attacks” is supported by the 2021 study. Do not convert this into “twice as many fish survive.”
- The 2026 kingfisher edge-targeting result is from a field study of three bird species, not a rule that every predator always attacks the edge.
- The exact reason the wave deters birds remains unresolved; do not say “the birds are definitely confused.”
